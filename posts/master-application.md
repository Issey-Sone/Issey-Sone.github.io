# My Master's application experience (Statistics)

*Jun 2026 · grad school* 

If you are reading this, you are likely interested in applying to grad school. 
Whether it be master's or PhD there is an entire application process to go through.
In this post I will try to explain my experience, thoughts, and resources I used
for this cycle of applications. Remember that grad school applications
are very subject specific. Applying to grad school for linguistics vs.
theoretical physics will be very different. It will be more helpful to talk with 
professors or other student's that are currently in the programs you are interested in.
I studied statistics in undergrad, so my own experiences will likely be more useful to you 
if you are from a similar background (Statistics, Mathematics, Computer Science, etc). 
Again, be sure to do your own research but I hope this can help some of you guys out (especially if you are from UTSC!). 

## Why Grad School?

This is where you should start, is doing a Master's or PhD worth it for you? What do you hope to gain after completing
that program. This will be important to think about as it will help you find specific programs you are looking for (There
are many master's programs out there!). This will also help you later down the line when you are required to write essays and
talk about your motivations for completing a graduate degree. 

## Master's vs PhD -- Which to choose? 
If you already know you want to do a PhD, feel free to skip this section. This will help if you are stuck between applying to a 
Master's or a PhD. You don't need a master's to be accepted into a PhD, many students are able to get into PhD programs directly 
from an undergraduate degree. Especially if you have high marks (3.8+ GPA), research experience, and generally know what research direction you want to go in, you should absolutely try for a PhD. There are also other factors like cost that might influence your decision too. If your record 
as an undergrad wasn't strong, and you want to do a PhD a master's can degree can help you become a stronger applicant. Looking back, I only applied to Master's programs but I wish I applied to a few PhD ones as well.

## Finding a Master's program
Master's programs vary quite a lot, master's degrees can be very domain specific as well. It's important to ask yourself *Why do I want to pursure graduate studies?*, and *What do I hope to gain after attending this program?*. If you are able to answer these two questions, then finding program's will be much easier. For me, I find programs falling under two categories: 
either a thesis-based one, or an industry-based one. Thesis-based master's programs will usually take 2 years to complete and has a thesis
requirement to graduate. These programs are tailored to students who want to go into a PhD in the future. Industry-based programs usually consists of courses to complete the degree. These programs are tailored to students who want to go into the industry, or perhaps students who are interested in a career change if their undergraduate degree was in something different. That being said, students who complete a thesis-based masters can get into industry, and industry-based masters can get into PhD programs too! This is evident if you look through people on LinkedIn and look through their educational background. 

## GRE -- Do I need it?
The GRE (Graduate Record Exam) is a standardized test owned and administered by ETS. I recommend taking the GRE if your program requires it, or strongly advises it. Doing well on the GRE can boost your application if your track record as an undergrad isn't particularly strong. If you just finished your 3rd year of undergraduate studies, and will start applying later in the fall. I highly recommend studying and taking the GRE during the summer. Not only do you have more time to study, you also give yourself another chance to take the exam again later in the fall if you weren't happy with your first score. Studying for the GRE can be quite tiring and mentally draining but it's important to be consistent with your study plans. I only started GRE preperation in September, and balancing school work along with GRE prep can be a lot to manage. I only spent 1.5 months studying for it and ended up with a 321 (168 quant, 153 verbal). While it isn't a horrible score, with more preparation and better time management, I believe I could have had a stronger result. I only used [**GregMat's**](https://www.gregmat.com/) GRE preperation videos to prepare for the exam and used the 1-month plan. It is more affordable than other GRE prep services and his techniques and videos are quite useful. In addition to the GRE prep, he also has dedicated personal statement writing videos which I highly recommend watching. Lastly, for the vocabulary and flash cards I highly recommend using Anki for this. There are many pre-made decks which you can find on the internet. 

## Reference Letters
A very important part of your application will be reference letters. Most programs will ask for 3 reference letters and ideally they come from full-time faculty at a University. While every other part of your application (GPA, coursework, essays) are an introspective view of your potential as a graduate student, the reference letter is the only part of your application that comes from an external view of your potential as a graduate student. They can talk about your character, traits and how you work as a student/researcher. All of these qualities are very important for a graduate committee to look at. 

Ideally, you want to ask three professors that you have worked with cloesly and understand how you work and communicate. If this isn't the case, think about courses that you may have taken and performed well on, and provide some samples of your work to help them write a letter for you. In this case, you want your referee to understand your work and what you worked on so that they can write about it. Make sure to ask well in advance, and think of back-up options if professors aren't able to write one for you. Assuming you give enough weeks notice, I find most professors were more than happy to write reference letters. (Don't be afraid of getting rejected!)

One advice a professor gave me was to apply to similar programs, if programs are drastically different then it makes it harder to write stronger reference letters. Be reasonable with the number of reference letters you need as well (don't apply to too many programs). 

## Essay writing
This section will be mainly based off the content given from Gregmat's 'Dedicated Personal Statements' class. There are many good sources out there and I will link some below. I highly recommend watching these series of videos. Most of what I mention here will be taken directly from Gregmat, and are just my own notes from these videos. 
### 1. Follow the directions 
While it sounds simple, it's quite easy to miss this step. Most program's will have their own dedicated prompt they want you to answer specifically. While it might be similar across school's, there are often slight nuances that should be observed. For example, the prompt\
*"Why do you want to be a PA?"* \
This question is simply asking you "what motivates/interests you in being a PA". Writing about the University is NOT following the directions. 
A helpful guide is to paraphrase what the prompt is asking, and break it down. Here is another example from Gregmat: \
\
*Please upload a statement of 500-1000 words explaining why you are applying to Yale for graduate study. Describe your research interests and preparation for your intended field of study, including prior research and other relevant experience. Relate how the faculty research, and faculty research would contribute to your future goals* \
\
Rephrase: What preparation you have done in undergraduate to prepare you for this masters in whatever you want to study. What excites you and what do you want to contribute? How would the faculty, and school help you in your goals? 

This process should be very procedural
+ 1. Am I answering the question?
+ 2. Organize my writing 
+ 3. Is it good writing, or a good response/answer?


### 2. Organize your work 
Planning Matters, Outlines Matter, and Narratives matter. \
There are several reasons why it can be hard to stay organized: 
+ 1. Don't follow the directions 
+ 2. Just writing before doing the plan
+ 3. Interpreting the prompts instead of answering 
+ 4. A lot of ideas, instead of organizing we just put it all on paper 
+ 5. We think the reader can infer what we mean

Remember, the clearer it is, the more thankful they(admissions comittee) will be. Here is a general guide, but make adjustments as necessary
as long as you are staying organized. 
1. Identify Main Thesis
	- Main thread running through your composition 
	- What are you arguing? 
2. Identify Paragraph topics
	- Self-contained mini composition 
	- What is each paragraph about? 
3. Identify Paragraph Links
	- They all have to be related in some way

And no you should not have headers for different sections such as "Introduction", "Why this school", "Why this program" etc.   

### 3. Be Honest, and be Vulnerable 
If you are writing a personal statement you want to show who you are as a person. You want to avoid hyping yourself up with a bunch of fluff, admissions comittee's can sense BS quite easily. Highlighting flaws makes you human too, talking about how perfect you are is cliche, and unrealistic. Think about your favorite characters from a movie or TV show, there is likely some flaw each character had that made that character memorable in some way. Another way to go about this is asking *What's your character arc?*. People love to see change, and often this can start from expressing doubts you have about your life and expressing the change afterwards. 

### 4. It's not really about you 
Focusing too much on dreams, and desires are mistakes in writing. How will you contribute to society? (It can't be BS), you have to maintain honesty here. Here are some examples: 
1. Advancing Literature: How will you move science forward? 
	- Doing research
	- benefit the field, society
	- dreams to do research is a good thing
2. Addressing modern problems: How will you help society overcome some of its issues? 
3. Helping the less privileged 
	- applying indicates some kind of privilege 
	- we acknowledge the reality of that, depends on luck 
4. Seeking the unknown 
	- Similar to advancing the literature, this is something I want to contribute giving an answer to

What are you *really* in it for? 
Think about what impact you want to give! It should be about value and perspective. Master's objectives could be worth looking at, and its a good idea to address what you will give back to society, local community, or even the institution itself. 

These were the 4 most helpful videos from Gregmat, and again, I highly recommend watching it yourself. Videos 5, and 6 were about *Powerful writing techniques*, *Proofreading* respectively. With proofreading I would make sure to have others read it too and see if they understand the message you are trying to convey as well.
